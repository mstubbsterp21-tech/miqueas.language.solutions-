Despite the legal and ethical mandates under the ADA and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, many healthcare organizations continue to make critical errors in how they facilitate communication. This guide outlines seven common mistakes and provides a pathway to fixing them.

1. Relying on Ad Hoc Interpreters (Family or Friends)

One of the most frequent: and dangerous: mistakes is asking a Deaf patient to bring a family member or friend to interpret. While it may seem convenient or "personal," it creates significant barriers to effective care.

The Risks of Ad Hoc Interpreters:

  1. Lack of Neutrality: Family members are emotionally involved. They may omit distressing information or filter the doctor’s words to "protect" the patient, leading to an incomplete clinical picture.
  2. Confidentiality Breaches: Using a family member violates the patient’s right to privacy and can lead to HIPAA complications.
  3. Accuracy Deficits: Professional medical terminology is complex. A "friend who knows some signs" lacks the specialized vocabulary and linguistic modality required to convey nuanced medical concepts.

The Fix: Always hire a qualified interpreter. Professional interpreters are bound by ethics of confidentiality and impartiality, ensuring the patient’s autonomy is respected.

2. Using VRI as a "One-Size-Fits-All" Solution

Video Remote Interpreting (VRI) is a valuable tool for specific virtual settings and telehealth, but it is not a universal replacement for on-site support.

When VRI Fails:

  • Poor Technical Infrastructure: Dropped connections, small screens, or poor lighting can render VRI useless.
  • Physical Limitations: If a patient is lying down, has limited mobility, or is in physical distress, they may not be able to see the screen or the interpreter clearly.
  • High-Stakes Moments: For terminal diagnoses, complex surgical consents, or mental health assessments, the lack of a physical presence can impede the necessary human connection and cultural responsiveness.

The Fix: Perform a modality fit assessment. If the encounter is high-stakes or the patient has physical constraints, prioritize in-person interpreting.

3. Neglecting Pre-Assignment Preparation

Interpreting is not a passive act of word-for-word translation; it is a cognitive process that requires context. Many providers fail to provide the interpreter with relevant materials before the appointment begins.

Why Context Matters:

  • Vocabulary Prep: Specific specialties (e.g., oncology, neurology) have unique terminology that requires mental preparation.
  • Flow of the Encounter: Knowing whether the visit is a routine check-up or a diagnostic result delivery allows the interpreter to prepare for the appropriate tone and pacing.

The Fix: Provide "readiness materials." Sharing the general nature of the appointment or any specific educational handouts beforehand allows the interpreter to maintain the highest standard of accuracy.

4. Prioritizing Cost Over Qualification

5. Overlooking the Physical Environment

The physical layout of a room can make or break communication access. ASL is a visual language, yet many providers ignore the basic requirements for visibility.

Environmental Barriers:

  • Backlighting: Standing in front of a bright window turns the provider into a silhouette, making it impossible for the patient to see facial expressions or signs.
  • Poor Sightlines: Placing the interpreter behind the patient or off to the side forces the patient to constantly twist their neck, leading to physical discomfort and missed information.

The Fix: Position the interpreter next to the healthcare provider. This allows the patient to see both the provider and the interpreter in the same field of vision, facilitating a more natural and inclusive conversation.

6. Addressing the Interpreter, Not the Patient

It is a common habit for healthcare providers to look at the interpreter and say, "Tell her to take this twice a day." This sidelines the patient and disrupts the provider-patient relationship.

The Role of the Interpreter:

The interpreter is a communication conduit, not a participant in the conversation. By addressing the interpreter, you diminish the patient's role as the primary decision-maker in their own care.

The Fix: Use first-person language and maintain eye contact with the patient. Say, "I need you to take this medicine twice a day." The interpreter will convey your message exactly as spoken, while the patient feels directly engaged in the care process.

Helpful Guidance: Accuracy in healthcare isn't just about the words: it's about the connection. Speaking directly to the patient ensures they remain at the center of the clinical encounter.

7. Ending Communication Access Prematurely

Mistakenly, many providers assume the interpreter is only needed while the doctor is in the room. This ignores critical parts of the patient journey.

Forgotten Touchpoints:

  • Registration and Intake: Collecting insurance information and medical history.
  • Nursing Assessments: Routine checks that often involve important patient questions.
  • Discharge and Pharmacy: Explaining follow-up care and medication instructions.

The Fix: Plan for the "entire continuum of care." Ensure the interpreter remains available from the moment the patient arrives until they have successfully checked out or been admitted.
