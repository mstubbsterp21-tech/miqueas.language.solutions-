White Cane Awareness Day is observed on October 15. The day recognizes the white cane as a tool of independence for people who are blind or have low vision. It also provides an opportunity to examine the broader access responsibilities that affect DeafBlind people, including communication access.

DeafBlind access is not one service or one communication method. It is a planning process based on the individual’s vision, hearing, language, experience, environment, and stated preferences.

For organizations arranging ASL interpreter services, “ASL requested” is not enough information. The assignment may require tactile interpreting, close-vision interpreting, a Support Service Provider (SSP), or a combination of supports. The correct plan begins with fit and readiness rather than simple availability.

DeafBlind is a spectrum, not a single profile

The term DeafBlind includes people with different combinations of hearing and vision loss. Some people use ASL and have limited usable vision. Others receive language through touch. Some use speech, residual hearing, braille, print, technology, or more than one method depending on the setting.

The person’s preferred communication method may also change based on:

  1. Lighting and glare.
  2. The distance between participants.
  3. Fatigue or health conditions.
  4. The number of people in the room.
  5. Whether the interaction is one-to-one or group-based.
  6. The complexity and pace of the information.
  7. Whether visual descriptions or environmental information are necessary.

A DeafBlind consumer may use one method during a medical appointment and another during a public meeting. A person who uses close-vision signing in a well-lit room may need tactile communication in a dim conference space.

Helpful guidance: Do not build an access plan from a diagnostic label. Build it from the person’s communication preferences and the conditions of the assignment.

Three communication approaches require different preparation

1. Tactile interpreting

In tactile interpreting, the DeafBlind consumer receives signed language through touch. The consumer may place their hands over the interpreter’s hands or wrists to perceive signs and movement.

Tactile interpreting requires deliberate attention to:

  • Clear handshapes and controlled movement.
  • Consistent physical contact.
  • Reduced or adapted signing space.
  • Explicit turn-taking.
  • Appropriate pauses for tactile processing.
  • Access to relevant visual and environmental information.

Tactile interpreting is not simply visual ASL performed closer to the consumer. It requires knowledge of how language, spatial information, transitions, and feedback are received through touch.

2. Close-vision interpreting

Close-vision interpreting supports a person who uses visual signing but has a restricted or changing visual field. The interpreter may need to sign within a specific distance, angle, size, or area.

Preparation may include questions about:

  • Preferred seating distance.
  • Central or peripheral vision.
  • Lighting and glare.
  • Contrast between the interpreter’s clothing and background.
  • Sign size and movement range.
  • Whether the consumer needs additional spoken, tactile, or written cues.

Positioning is part of the service. An interpreter placed outside the consumer’s usable field is not providing effective access, even if the interpreter is otherwise qualified.

3. Protactile approaches

Protactile approaches are DeafBlind-centered ways of sharing information through touch, movement, and shared physical space. Protactile is not simply a visual language converted into touch. It may include tactile signals that communicate audience reaction, movement, spatial relationships, attention, or other information available through the environment.

Not every DeafBlind person uses Protactile. The interpreter or SSP should not assume familiarity or impose a method. The consumer’s stated preference remains the controlling factor.

Preference determines the method, not convenience

An organization may prefer the method that is easiest to schedule or most familiar to staff. That preference does not determine effective communication access.

The consumer should be asked directly:

  1. What communication method do you prefer for this setting?
  2. Do you use tactile, close-vision, Protactile, spoken, written, braille, or combined methods?
  3. Where should the interpreter or communication partner sit or stand?
  4. How do you prefer to receive turn-taking cues?
  5. Do you need descriptions of people, room changes, visual reactions, or printed materials?
  6. Do lighting, background, distance, or physical contact affect your access?
  7. Does the assignment require an SSP in addition to an interpreter?

These questions are not administrative details. They determine whether the service is usable.

Interpreter and SSP roles are different

An interpreter’s primary role is to facilitate communication between languages or communication modalities. The interpreter conveys the messages of the DeafBlind consumer and other participants while maintaining professional boundaries, accuracy, confidentiality, and impartiality.

An SSP provides support that may include:

  • Guiding and orientation.
  • Describing environmental information.
  • Identifying people, movement, signage, and physical layout.
  • Supporting navigation through a building or public space.
  • Relaying choices without making decisions for the consumer.
  • Helping the consumer access information outside the direct exchange of language.

An SSP is not a substitute for an interpreter when the assignment requires language interpretation. An interpreter is not automatically a substitute for an SSP when the consumer needs guiding, environmental information, or navigation support.

When an assignment needs an interpreter

An interpreter is generally needed when the consumer must participate in communication involving:

  • A medical consultation.
  • A legal or administrative meeting.
  • A school or workplace discussion.
  • A public presentation.
  • A service-provider appointment.
  • A complex exchange involving informed decisions.

When an assignment needs an SSP

An SSP may be needed when the primary requirement involves:

  • Navigating an unfamiliar environment.
  • Receiving information about surroundings.
  • Identifying people and physical changes.
  • Moving safely through a public event.
  • Accessing visual or auditory information outside a direct conversation.

When both are needed

Both professionals may be appropriate when an assignment combines complex communication with substantial environmental or mobility demands. A large conference, hospital visit, community event, or unfamiliar public facility may require separate language access and support services.

Longer, tactile, or high-context assignments may also require team staffing. Tactile work increases physical and cognitive demand. The professionals must sustain contact, process language through a different channel, manage turn-taking, monitor accuracy, and account for environmental information. Staffing decisions should be based on assignment demands, not only duration.

Environmental preparation is part of communication access

Before the assignment, the organization and service provider should review the physical environment.

Lighting and background

Close-vision communication may be affected by glare, shadows, insufficient light, or a visually busy background. Tactile communication may require enough light for introductions, orientation, documents, or transitions between methods.

Seating and positioning

The consumer should help determine where people sit. The arrangement may need to support:

  • Direct access to the interpreter’s hands.
  • A clear visual field.
  • Awareness of other participants.
  • Access to a table, armrest, or stable surface.
  • Safe movement into and out of the room.

Physical space

Keep pathways clear. Do not move furniture, personal items, or equipment without informing the consumer. A change in room layout can affect orientation and safety.

Turn-taking cues

A DeafBlind consumer may not see a raised hand, a person turning toward them, or a speaker entering the conversation. The access plan should identify how the consumer receives turn-taking information. This may involve tactile cues, a stated name, a consistent signal, or a Protactile convention preferred by the consumer.

Time and pacing

DeafBlind assignments generally require more time. The consumer may need additional processing time, physical repositioning, repeated information, or explicit identification of a new speaker. Participants should pause before responding and avoid overlapping conversations.

Intake: why “ASL requested” is not enough

A useful intake process asks about the actual communication conditions. At minimum, an organization should document:

  1. The consumer’s preferred communication modality.
  2. Whether the consumer uses tactile, close-vision, Protactile, or combined approaches.
  3. Whether an interpreter, SSP, or both are requested.
  4. Assignment length, subject matter, and number of participants.
  5. Room size, lighting, seating, and expected movement.
  6. Documents, presentations, or specialized vocabulary.
  7. Guiding, navigation, or environmental information needs.
  8. Whether team staffing may be necessary.
  9. The preferred method for turn-taking and attention-getting.
  10. Any boundaries around touch, privacy, or physical contact.

A request that says only “ASL requested” leaves critical questions unanswered. It may result in an interpreter who cannot be perceived, a method the consumer does not use, or an assignment where no one is prepared to provide environmental support.

Common failures to avoid

Organizations can prevent many access problems by avoiding these assumptions:

  • Assuming every DeafBlind person reads large print.
  • Assuming every DeafBlind person uses tactile signing.
  • Placing the interpreter where the consumer cannot perceive them.
  • Treating an SSP and interpreter as interchangeable.
  • Scheduling only one professional for a physically demanding assignment.
  • Failing to describe who has entered or left the room.
  • Expecting the consumer to follow rapid turn-taking without support.
  • Providing an interpreter without asking how the consumer prefers to receive information.
  • Treating environmental preparation as optional.
  • Measuring success by whether an interpreter was present rather than whether the consumer could participate.

Accuracy requires judgment. Judgment requires preparation. The consumer’s access plan should be specific enough to guide the people responsible for carrying it out.

A practical planning pathway

For organizations arranging DeafBlind communication access:

  1. Ask the consumer about preferred modality and support needs.
  2. Share the assignment purpose, setting, schedule, and materials.
  3. Determine whether the request requires an interpreter, SSP, or both.
  4. Review lighting, seating, physical space, and movement.
  5. Assess whether tactile work, complexity, or duration calls for team staffing.
  6. Confirm how turn-taking, introductions, and environmental information will be handled.
  7. Allow additional time for processing and transitions.
  8. Reconfirm the plan before the assignment begins.

No commitment is required to start the review. Clear next steps begin with complete information and a willingness to ask the right questions.

Frequently Asked Questions

How should an organization document a DeafBlind consumer’s changing access needs over time?

Maintain an access profile that records the consumer’s stated preferences without treating it as permanent. Confirm the profile before significant assignments, especially when the location, participants, schedule, health conditions, or communication demands change. The consumer should be able to update or correct the information directly.

What should a provider do when a DeafBlind consumer uses different methods with different communication partners?

Document the method preferred for the specific assignment rather than selecting one general method for every situation. Share only the information necessary for the professionals involved, and confirm whether the consumer wants the interpreter, SSP, or both to switch methods during the encounter.

How can an organization include DeafBlind access in emergency and evacuation planning?

Ask the consumer and appropriate support professionals how alerts, directions, evacuation routes, changing hazards, and regrouping information should be communicated. Build those procedures into the venue plan before an emergency occurs, and avoid assuming that visual alarms or general announcements provide usable access.
