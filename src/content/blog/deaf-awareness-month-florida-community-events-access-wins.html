Four communication access wins organizations can adopt

Deaf Awareness Month does not require every organization to create a large event. A smaller operational improvement can produce a more reliable result.

1. Publish access information before registration

Do not make attendees search for basic access details. Include the following in the event announcement:

  • Whether an ASL interpreter will be present
  • Whether live captions or CART are available
  • How to request additional accommodations
  • The deadline for access requests
  • A direct contact method
  • Whether the event includes videos, audience participation, or technical demonstrations

This approach benefits healthcare providers, schools, businesses, and community organizations. It also gives Deaf attendees enough information to decide whether the event meets their communication needs.

2. Match the modality to the setting

“Interpreter requested” is not a complete assignment description. Organizations should identify the setting and communication requirements before requesting service.

Consider:

  • In-person or remote delivery
  • Number of participants
  • Duration and schedule
  • Specialized terminology
  • Lighting and sightlines
  • Audience size
  • Whether materials will be presented on screen
  • Whether the interaction involves sensitive or high-stakes information

A healthcare facility may need sign language services for healthcare for a consultation involving technical vocabulary and multiple providers. A company may need ASL interpreting for businesses for a meeting with presentations, discussion, and remote participants.

The right modality depends on the communication task, not simply on what is easiest to schedule.

3. Build visual access into the event workflow

Interpreting is only one part of access. Attendees also need to know when the program starts, when the agenda changes, and where to go next.

Organizations can improve the experience by:

  1. Displaying the agenda in a visible location.
  2. Using visual alerts for schedule changes.
  3. Keeping presenters’ faces and interpreters visible.
  4. Avoiding backlighting and poor stage lighting.
  5. Sending presentation materials in advance when possible.
  6. Assigning one staff member to monitor access logistics.
  7. Explaining how attendees can ask questions or request clarification.

These actions reduce dependence on incidental announcements and support participants who rely on visual communication.

4. Review the event with Deaf participants

An access plan should be evaluated by the people who use it. After the program, invite specific feedback about:

  • Registration and pre-event information
  • Interpreter fit and visibility
  • Captions or visual materials
  • Staff responsiveness
  • Room layout and lighting
  • Timing of breaks
  • Whether participants could ask questions and contribute fully

Feedback should lead to a documented change for the next event. Cultural responsiveness includes listening without treating feedback as a personal criticism.

Planning pathway for Florida organizations

Use this four-step process for a September program or any future assignment.

1. Define the communication need

Identify who will participate, what information will be exchanged, and what level of access is required. “Deaf Awareness Month event” is not enough detail for responsible planning.

2. Complete a fit and readiness review

Review the setting, modality, schedule, subject matter, materials, and participant needs. A professional ASL interpreter should be selected based on assignment fit, preparation, and readiness rather than availability alone.

3. Share materials early

Provide agendas, presentations, terminology, names, policies, scripts, and other relevant information as early as possible. Preparation supports accuracy and judgment.

4. Confirm the access plan

Before the event, confirm arrival time, location, technology, interpreter visibility, contact information, and any changes to the agenda. If the assignment requires live ASL communication access, in-person interpreting or video remote interpreting may be appropriate depending on the setting.

FAQ

How should an organization handle an event where the Deaf audience uses more than one sign language or communication system?

Begin by asking the intended participants or community partners what language and communication preferences should guide the event. ASL should not be assumed to meet every attendee’s needs. The access plan may require consultation with Deaf-led organizations, multiple language resources, or a different communication strategy.

What should a Florida organization do if its event is announced before access services are confirmed?

Update the public notice with a clear access statement rather than implying that access is guaranteed. Assign one contact person to collect requests, document the current status, and communicate changes promptly. Avoid advertising “interpreters provided” until the service arrangement is confirmed.

How can an organization measure whether a Deaf Awareness Month initiative had a meaningful result?

Use evidence beyond attendance numbers. Review whether Deaf participants could obtain information, follow the program, participate in discussions, and provide feedback. Track the operational changes that remain after September, such as revised registration language, staff procedures, accessible materials, and a documented process for future ASL interpreter services.
